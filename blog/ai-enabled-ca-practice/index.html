Get safe and fluent first: what this tool actually is, how to instruct it, and what still needs your signature.

Sections 1–6
01

What AI is

A brilliant assistant who has never seen your files and never says "I don't know."

Ready
In plain words

ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are prediction engines trained on huge amounts of public text. They write fluently about law and accounts, but they know nothing about your clients and see nothing you don't show them. Think of one as a brilliant new articled assistant: superb drafting, zero context, always needs review.

In your practice

Ask "my client received an intimation under 143(1) — what is it?" and you get a clean, largely correct explanation. Ask "why did my client receive it?" and it cannot know. The gap between those two questions is what this guide closes.

Do this today
  1. Open ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini — free versions are enough to start.
  2. Ask one work question you already know the answer to.
  3. Note what it got right — and what it could not have known.

02

Strengths and limits

It reasons over what you give it. It invents what you ask it to recall.

Ready
In plain words

Reliably strong: summarising, drafting, restructuring and spotting issues in text you give it. Reliably weak: quoting sections, case law, dates and rates from memory — when unsure, it fills the gap with confident fiction (a "hallucination"). So feed it material to reason over; never use it as a database.

In your practice

Ask for case law supporting your stand on a Section 68 addition, and you may receive a judgment that does not exist — perfect citation format, plausible bench, entirely invented. Give the same model the actual judgment PDF and it summarises it flawlessly.

Do this today
  1. Paste a recent circular and ask for a five-point summary — note the quality.
  2. Ask it to cite case law from memory on any issue — then try to verify one citation.
  3. Write the house rule where the team can see it: "Paste, don't ask it to recall."
03

The prompt

Role, context, task, format, constraints. Five parts, better output.

Ready
In plain words

A prompt is just your instruction, and vague instructions get generic work — same as with a junior. Five parts fix it: Role (who it should be), Context (the facts), Task (what to do), Format (how the output should look), Constraints (what it must not do). You already brief articles this way.

In your practice

"Draft a reply to this GST notice" returns a generic essay. "You are a senior indirect-tax consultant + here are the SCN's allegations and our facts + draft a para-wise reply + numbered paragraphs with headings + rely only on the sections I've quoted" returns a usable first draft.

Do this today
  1. Take one task you would brief a junior on today.
  2. Write it as the five parts using the skeleton below.
  3. Run both versions — compare what changed.
Copy this prompt skeleton 
You are a [senior CA specialising in …].
Context: [client type, the facts, figures — paste relevant extracts below].
Task: [draft / summarise / check / compare …].
Format: [a table with columns … / numbered paragraphs / under 300 words].
Constraints: [use only the material provided; mark anything you are unsure of;
do not invent sections, case law or figures].
04

Your documents

Stop asking it to remember. Start asking it to read.

Ready
In plain words

Every major chatbot now accepts PDFs, Excel files and images. Upload the actual document instead of describing it: the AI reads instead of recalls, and error rates fall sharply. Your typed summary is a filter — the document is the facts.

In your practice

Upload the show-cause notice PDF and the relevant ledger extract, then ask for a point-wise summary of every allegation with the figures involved. Compare that to what you got last week by describing the notice from memory — it isn't close.

Do this today
  1. Pick one notice or agreement sitting on your desk (see section 6 before uploading client data).
  2. Upload it and ask for a point-wise summary plus the issues it raises.
  3. Verify the summary against the original, page by page.
05

Verification

The AI prepares. You still sign.

Ready
In plain words

AI moves your time from drafting to reviewing — it never moves the responsibility. The signature, the UDIN and the liability remain yours. So AI output gets exactly the review an article's work gets: traced to source before it leaves the firm.

In your practice

AI drafts your Form 3CD observations in seconds. You still open the file and verify every clause against the books before signing — nothing about your professional duty changed, only where your hours go.

Do this today
  1. Save the four-question review below where the team works.
  2. Run it on the last AI output you used.
  3. Make it the standing rule for anything that leaves the firm.
The four-question review
  • Figures — does every number trace to the source document?
  • Law — are sections, dates and citations verified against the bare act or portal?
  • Invention — is anything named (cases, notifications, facts) that you did not provide?
  • Voice — is the tone and format the firm's, not the machine's?
06

Confidentiality

The liability never moves to the software.

Ready
In plain words

Client confidentiality is your duty regardless of which software touches the data. Free chatbot plans may use conversations to train models — find the data-control setting and read the policy; paid business plans generally commit not to. Until you've done that, the habit is simple: anonymise by default.

In your practice

Before asking for analysis of a client's P&L, strip the name, PAN and GSTIN and call them "a mid-size textile trader". The quality of the analysis is identical. The exposure is not.

Do this today
  1. Open your AI tool's data / training settings and set them consciously.
  2. Adopt the one-line rule: anonymise first, paste second.
  3. Brief your team in one stand-up — the list below is enough.
What never goes in · what can

Never paste

PAN, Aadhaar, passwords, OTPs, login credentials, full statements with names, or your client list — into any public chatbot.

Safe with care

Anonymised figures, extracts with identifiers removed, your own templates and checklists, published law and circulars.

Sections 7–11
07

Templates

If you typed it twice, you built it wrong.

Ready
In plain words

The second time you type a similar prompt, stop and save it with blanks: [CLIENT], [SECTION], [FACTS]. A shared prompt library is your firm's first AI asset — output quality stops depending on who typed, and a partner-approved wording gets reused instead of reinvented.

In your practice

One saved notice-reply template serves every client: the blanks change, while the structure, tone and constraints your partner already approved stay fixed. The junior fills blanks instead of composing from scratch.

Do this today
  1. Find the best prompt you wrote this week.
  2. Replace the specifics with [BLANKS].
  3. Start the firm library — one shared doc, three entries, named by task.
08

Standing context

Load your firm once, not in every chat.

Ready
In plain words

Every serious AI tool has a place to save standing instructions — Projects, custom instructions, Gems. Write your firm's profile once (what you do, for whom, in what style) and every new chat starts already briefed. Stop re-typing "we are a CA firm…" forty times a month.

In your practice

A "GST notices" project holds your firm profile, your reply format and two of your best past replies (anonymised). Every chat inside it drafts in the house style automatically — the junior's chat and yours now start from the same place.

Do this today
  1. Fill the profile below in five minutes.
  2. Save it as custom instructions or a project.
  3. Re-run one old task inside it — compare the tone.
Your firm profile — fill and save 
About us: [firm size, cities, years in practice].
We serve: [client types — traders, manufacturers, startups, NRIs …].
Our work: [GST, statutory audit, income tax, advisory …].
House style: [formal English, numbered paragraphs, short sentences, no adjectives].
Always: [ask before assuming facts; list the sections you rely on].
Never: [invent case law or figures; calculate due dates — I provide them].
09

Structured output

Tables can be reviewed. Paragraphs can only be believed.

Ready
In plain words

Always dictate the shape of the answer: a table, fixed columns, numbered points. A table is checked row by row against source — that's review. A well-written paragraph can only be trusted or distrusted whole — that's faith. Structure is what turns AI output into a working paper.

In your practice

26AS versus books: "Give me a table — Particulars, As per books, As per 26AS, Difference, Action" — and you review twelve rows in two minutes. The same content as prose would need three careful readings.

Do this today
  1. Re-run yesterday's AI task, this time demanding a table.
  2. Add a "flag what's missing" instruction from the phrases below.
  3. Paste the format line into your templates from section 7.
Format phrases that work 
… as a table with columns: [Particulars | As per books | As per 26AS | Difference | Action].
… numbered points, one issue per point, the section relied on in brackets.
… flag every figure you could not find in the data I gave you — do not fill gaps.
10

Five workflows

Build once in the morning, use every month for a year.

Ready
In plain words

A workflow is simply template + standing context + fixed output format, aimed at a job you repeat every month. Build one in a morning, and next month the job starts at 80% done. Build five, and AI stops being a toy — it's part of the month's rhythm.

In your practice

The five worth building first: GSTR-2B vs purchase register commentary · TDS section/rate check on new payments · ledger scrutiny notes · MIS variance commentary · month-end client update mail. Each is monthly, text-heavy and format-stable — ideal AI material.

Do this today
  1. Pick the one of the five that eats the most team hours.
  2. Assemble it: template (s7) + firm profile (s8) + table format (s9).
  3. Test on last month's data, where you already know the answers.
11

Data without code

Ask for the formula, not the answer.

Ready
In plain words

For numbers, don't ask AI to compute — its arithmetic over long lists is unreliable. Ask for the method: the Excel formula, the pivot steps, the check to run. Your spreadsheet does the math, and every cell stays auditable. (Some tools can also write and run small Python scripts for you — same rule: you read the steps, the machine computes.)

In your practice

Bank reco: not "reconcile these two lists", but "give me the XLOOKUP setup and pivot steps to match bank statement entries to books, and the checks for items matched twice". Then run it in Excel where the result is traceable.

Do this today
  1. Take the Excel problem you google most often.
  2. Ask AI for the formula and the steps, explained in plain words.
  3. Apply it on a copy of the sheet; hand-verify three sample rows.

12

Knowledge base

Twenty years of judgment, currently scattered across folders.

Ready
In plain words

Your firm's real IP isn't software — it's judgment: the opinions that held, the replies that worked, the checklists refined over years. Today it lives in scattered folders and senior partners' heads. Step one of firm-level AI is unglamorous: collect it, name it consistently, keep it in one place.

In your practice

Your twenty best notice replies, standard checklists and opinion notes — filed once as "topic — FY — client type", anonymised — become the raw material every later system (retrieval in s13, agents in s15) runs on. Garbage folders in, garbage answers out.

Do this today
  1. Create the five folders below.
  2. Move in your ten best documents — best, not most recent.
  3. Write the naming convention as one line in the top folder.
Folder structure to copy 
KnowledgeBase/
  01-Opinions/        (topic — FY — client type, anonymised)
  02-NoticeReplies/   (section — outcome — FY)
  03-Checklists/      (task — last reviewed date)
  04-Formats/         (letters, reports, working papers)
  05-SOPs/            (how the firm does each recurring job)
13

Retrieval

Answers from your own files, with the page number attached.

Ready
In plain words

Retrieval (the technical name is RAG) means the AI answers only from a library you give it — your knowledge base — and attaches the source it used. The citation is the whole point: you verify a page, not a paragraph of prose. Tools like NotebookLM or Projects do this with zero code.

In your practice

"What did we advise on 194Q applicability in FY 23–24?" — answered from your own opinion file, with the file name and page attached, verified in one click. New joiners stop re-deriving what the firm already knows.

Do this today
  1. Upload five anonymised knowledge-base documents to NotebookLM (or a project).
  2. Ask three questions you already know the answers to.
  3. Click every citation — make source-checking the reflex from day one.

14

Tool connections

Read widely. Write only into drafts.

Ready
In plain words

AI tools can now connect to your Drive, mail and accounting exports and work across them. Treat the two directions very differently: reading widely is low-risk and where most of the value is; writing is where a mistake becomes permanent. So connections read broadly, and write only into places you review.

In your practice

AI reads the client folder and Tally exports to prepare the month's summary — freely. Everything it writes lands in a folder literally named "Drafts — review before use". Nothing reaches a client, the books or a portal without your eyes on it.

Do this today
  1. List what your AI tools can currently read and write.
  2. Create the "Drafts — review before use" folder.
  3. Put the rule below into your one-page AI policy.
The read–write rule
  • Read — allow broadly: folders, exports, mail. This is where the value is.
  • Write — drafts only, in one named folder a human always opens.
  • Send / file / submit — never automated. A person clicks, every time.

15

Agents

The design decision is not what it does. It is where it stops.

Ready
In plain words

An agent is AI that chains several steps on its own — read the document, pull references, draft, assemble — instead of waiting for your next message. The design question is not "what can it do" but "where must it stop for a human". You place the stop where an error would be expensive.

In your practice

A notice-reply agent: reads the notice, pulls your three most similar past replies from the knowledge base, drafts the response, lists the annexures needed — then stops. Reviewing is yours. Sending is yours. That stop is the design.

Do this today
  1. Write one repetitive job as a numbered list of steps.
  2. Mark the first step where a mistake would be costly.
  3. That's your human checkpoint — automate before it, never past it.
16

Rules and verification

Never let a model calculate a due date.

Ready
In plain words

Split your work in two. Judgment work — drafting, analysis, explanation — is where AI helps. Rule work — due dates, rates, thresholds, interest — must come from a lookup: your master sheet, the portal, a formula. A model that "calculates" a due date is guessing, even when it's right.

In your practice

Your workflows quote TDS rates and due dates from a master sheet you attach, with the instruction "take rates and dates only from the master". If an output shows a date you didn't supply, that's a design bug to fix — not a lucky save to ignore.

Do this today
  1. Build or update one master sheet: due dates, TDS rates, limits.
  2. Add the "only from the master" line to your templates.
  3. Spot-check one workflow output against the master.
Always a rule, never the model
  • Due dates and extension notifications
  • Tax & TDS rates, cess, surcharge
  • Thresholds and limits — audit, registration, presumptive
  • Interest and late-fee computations
  • Client master data — PANs, GSTINs, periods, jurisdictions
17

Automation platforms

You don't need to write code. You need to read it.

Ready
In plain words

Platforms like n8n, Zapier and Make chain your apps into flows: mail arrives → data extracted → sheet updated → task created. You build by choosing steps, not writing code. Your professional duty is the same as with any working paper: read every step and know exactly what runs when.

In your practice

A new-client email triggers: onboarding checklist copied, client folder created from your structure, tasks assigned, welcome letter drafted into the Drafts folder. Five readable steps replace an hour of setup — and you can audit them like a process note.

Do this today
  1. Open n8n (or Zapier/Make) and its template gallery.
  2. Read one accounting-adjacent template step by step, out loud.
  3. Sketch one of your own processes as steps on paper — that's the build plan.

18

AI advisory

Your clients are already using AI without controls. That gap is billable.

Ready
In plain words

Your clients' accountants are already pasting company data into free chatbots — with no policy, no anonymisation, no review habit. Everything you built in Parts A–C (settings, rules, checklists, workflows) is exactly what they're missing. That is a service line, not a side effect.

In your practice

A paid "AI usage review": where the client's team uses AI, what data leaks, which tasks are safe, a one-page policy, an hour of staff training. You already road-tested every piece of it on your own firm.

Do this today
  1. Add one question to your next client meeting: "Is your team using ChatGPT with company data?"
  2. Write down the gaps you hear — they repeat across clients.
  3. Draft your one-page "AI usage review" offer from Parts A–C.
19

Agentic finance

The work doesn't vanish. It moves up the stack.

Ready
In plain words

As agents mature, first-pass work — entry, matching, first drafts — compresses hard. What grows is everything above it: review, exceptions, judgment, explaining what the numbers mean. The firms that own the review layer keep the client relationship, and the fee moves with the relationship.

In your practice

Where a junior spent the week on data entry and first-pass reco, a system now produces the first pass. Your team reviews exceptions, resolves them, and walks the client through the story. Headcount shifts from typing to thinking — plan roles for that, not against it.

Do this today
  1. List your services by how much is routine first-pass work.
  2. Mark what systems could plausibly draft within a year.
  3. Write the review-and-advice role your team plays on each — that's your staffing plan.
20

Branding

Publish what you have built, not what you have read.

Ready
In plain words

Clients and good hires can't see your systems unless you show them. The feed is full of CAs resharing AI headlines; almost nobody shows a working build. One post about something you actually run says more than fifty reposts — it signals process, safety and modernity at once.

In your practice

"How our firm reviews AI-drafted GST replies — our four-point check" with a screenshot of the checklist. That single post outperforms a month of shared news links, because it's yours.

Do this today
  1. Pick one thing you built while following this guide.
  2. Write 150 words: the problem → what you built → what changed.
  3. Post it where your clients look. Repeat monthly, not daily.
21

90-day roadmap

Thirty days to habit, thirty to templates, thirty to systems.

Ready
In plain words

Ninety days, three gears, twenty minutes a day. You don't need a transformation project — you need a habit that compounds into templates, and templates that compound into systems. The three phases below map exactly onto Parts A, B and C of this guide.

In your practice

Run it alongside a normal month — the plan assumes filing deadlines, audits and everything else. Only two half-day "build" sessions are needed: one around day 45, one around day 75.

Do this today
  1. Block 20 minutes daily for the next 30 days.
  2. Put the two build sessions (day ~45, day ~75) in your calendar now.
  3. Tell one partner or colleague the plan — accountability works.
The 90 days
Days 1–30 · HabitOne real work task through AI daily. Verify everything. Anonymise by default. (Part A)
Days 31–60 · TemplatesFive templates saved. Firm profile set. Tables, not essays. (Part B)
Days 61–90 · SystemsKnowledge base + retrieval running. One monthly workflow live with a human checkpoint. Publish one build. (Parts C–D)
22

Risk register

Seven failures that look exactly like good work.

Ready
In plain words

None of these failures look like failures — each arrives polished, confident and formatted. That's precisely what makes them dangerous to a busy firm. Treat the register like any audit risk: named, owned, reviewed monthly.

In your practice

Print the seven rows. In your monthly ops or partner review, ask one question per row: "did our checks catch this class of error this month?" Ten minutes, once a month, is the entire control.

Do this today
  1. Print the register below.
  2. Add it to the monthly review agenda.
  3. Assign one named owner per risk — unowned risks drift.

Self-assessment

Eleven boxes. Tick them honestly and you know your level.

Ready
In plain words

Eleven honest ticks place your practice on the ladder from chatbot user to system-run firm. The score matters less than the first unticked box — that box is literally your next section to work on.

In your practice

Take it today for a baseline, note the score somewhere, and retake it on day 90 of the roadmap. The distance between the two numbers is your progress, in writing.

Do this today
  1. Tick the boxes honestly — nobody is watching.
  2. Find your first unticked box; that's this week's work.
  3. Diary a re-take for day 90.
Your eleven boxes

0–3 Curious · 4–6 User · 7–9 Builder · 10–11 System-led. Boxes 1–6 are Part A, 7–9 Part B, 10–11 Part C.
